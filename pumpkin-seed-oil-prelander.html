Hair & hormones

Five things happening to your hair through menopause

Your hair didn't start thinning because you got older. It started because one hormone dropped — and that changes what your follicles do far more than age does.

By the Kaizen Culture team·Updated ·8 min read

Most women notice it in the shower first. Not a dramatic handful — just more strands than there used to be, consistently, for months. Then the ponytail gets thinner. Then the part gets wider.

And almost everyone reaches the same wrong conclusion: this is just what happens when you get older. It isn't, exactly. Age is a bystander. What's driving the change is a hormonal shift with a specific mechanism — and once you understand it, a lot of things that didn't work start to make sense.

Perimenopause, menopause and postmenopause aren't three separate things happening to your hair. They're three stages of the same hormonal event: the decline of estrogen.

1

The shedding tracks estrogen, not birthdays

Estrogen helps keep hair in its growing phase. As levels fall, that phase gets shorter — follicles spend more time resting and shedding, less time producing. Meanwhile the androgens that were always present don't fall as sharply, so the ratio shifts. In follicles that are genetically sensitive to it, usually along the part and crown, that shift drives miniaturisation: each cycle produces a slightly finer, shorter hair than before.

This is why it shows up as widening rather than bald patches. The follicles aren't dying. They're downshifting.

That distinction is the part almost nobody explains. A miniaturised follicle is still alive and still cycling. Most menopausal thinning is this kind — which is exactly why the timeline further down matters.

2

Why hair vitamins so often do nothing

Biotin is in nearly every hair supplement on the shelf. It works — if you're deficient, which is rare on an ordinary diet. For most women a biotin supplement is correcting a problem they don't have. The same logic covers most of the shelf: if the driver is a hormonal ratio shift, adding nutrients that weren't missing doesn't address it. You haven't done anything wrong. You've been handed a solution aimed at a different problem.

Worth ruling out first

Thyroid dysfunction, iron deficiency and certain medications also cause diffuse hair loss, and they're treatable. A ferritin and thyroid panel from your doctor is cheap and worth doing before you assume it's hormonal. Nothing on this page is a substitute for that.

3

Where pumpkin seed oil comes in

Cold-pressed pumpkin seed oil is unusually rich in phytosterols — plant compounds structurally similar to cholesterol, with beta-sitosterol the most studied. It also carries zinc, vitamin E and omega-6 fatty acids. The interest for hair comes from laboratory work suggesting beta-sitosterol may influence 5-alpha reductase, the enzyme in the pathway that shifts the androgen balance. Given that menopausal thinning involves that same balance, the mechanism is at least pointed at the right target.

To date, one small randomized controlled trial has looked at pumpkin seed oil and hair directly: Cho and colleagues (2014) gave 76 men with mild-to-moderate pattern hair loss 400mg of pumpkin seed oil daily for 24 weeks, and the oil group saw a mean 40% increase in hair count versus 10% in the placebo group. That study was in men, at a lower dose than a full serving here, so we don't present it as a promise for women — we share it because it's the direct evidence that exists, and we'd rather you see it plainly. Separately, in the population this product is made for, a randomized, double-blind trial gave 35 postmenopausal women 2g of pumpkin seed oil a day for 12 weeks; it reported improvements in HDL cholesterol, blood pressure and menopausal symptom scores — though notably, it did not measure hair. Pumpkin seed oil is a food supplement, not a drug, and it isn't a substitute for minoxidil, finasteride or a conversation with your doctor. What it is: a mechanistically sensible, food-first option worth giving an honest three to four months.

4

What a realistic timeline actually looks like

Hair grows on a fixed biological schedule and nothing shortens it. A follicle that shifts back toward growing this month is still producing hair at roughly a centimetre a month, and that hair still has to clear the scalp before you can see it.

Weeks 1–4

Nothing visible yet

Follicles already in their shedding phase complete it regardless. This is normal and expected.

Weeks 5–8

Shedding may slow

The earliest point at which a change in how much you're losing could plausibly be noticed.

Weeks 9–12

Baby hairs possible

New growth begins reaching a length you can see and feel along the part and hairline.

Months 4–6

Visible thickness

Enough new growth has cleared the scalp to read as fuller. This is when most people decide whether to continue.

Any product promising visible regrowth in three weeks is selling against human biology. Understanding the timeline is genuinely protective — it stops you abandoning something at week six that was never going to show until month four.

Questions

How long before I see anything?

Realistically three to four months before visible change, based on how fast hair grows. Anyone promising faster is not describing biology.

Can I take this alongside HRT?

Ask your doctor. HRT is a well-established treatment for menopausal symptoms and this is a food supplement — they answer different questions, and one is not a replacement for the other. This is not a substitute for hormone therapy or for medical advice about it. Bring the label to your next appointment.

Will it help if my thinning isn't hormonal?

Probably not, and that's worth knowing before you buy. Thyroid issues, low ferritin and some medications cause similar shedding through different routes. Get those ruled out first.
